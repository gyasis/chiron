# Cadences in Tonal Harmony

Cadences are harmonic resting points — the punctuation marks of a musical phrase. The most common are the authentic cadence (V→I), the plagal cadence (IV→I), the deceptive cadence (V→vi), and the half cadence (any chord→V). Authentic cadences feel resolved and conclusive, like a period at the end of a sentence; they anchor most Western music's strongest arrival points. Plagal cadences feel reverent and gentle, which is why they are often called the "amen" cadence in hymns. Deceptive cadences subvert expectation: the ear is led to expect a tonic resolution after the dominant, but the music sidesteps to vi instead, opening space for continuation. Half cadences leave the listener suspended on V, demanding eventual resolution. The choice of cadence shapes a phrase's emotional arc and the listener's sense of closure or onward motion. Composers exploit these patterns deliberately — placing a deceptive cadence just before a true authentic cadence, for example, intensifies the eventual arrival. Recognizing cadence types by ear is one of the foundational skills of tonal listening, because they reveal the underlying chord-progression logic that organizes phrases into balanced musical sentences.
