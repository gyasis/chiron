## Abstract

**Background.** Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) reduce HbA1c and body weight in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and several large cardiovascular outcome trials (CVOTs) have suggested cardiovascular (CV) benefit. The magnitude of effect on CV mortality across the full contemporary trial portfolio — including primary-prevention populations and oral agents — remains uncertain.

**Methods.** We conducted a systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of randomized, placebo-controlled trials of GLP-1 RAs in adults with T2DM, with a primary outcome of cardiovascular mortality. We searched MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane CENTRAL register, and ClinicalTrials.gov from January 2010 to December 2024. Two reviewers independently screened, extracted data, and assessed risk of bias using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool. Pooled hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals were computed using DerSimonian–Laird random-effects models. Pre-specified subgroup analyses included baseline CV disease status, dosing schedule, and route of administration. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024-FIXTURE-0001).

**Results.** Of 8,642 records identified, 12 trials enrolling 87,432 patients met inclusion criteria. The benefit was numerically larger in patients with established CV disease (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.74–0.93) than in primary-prevention populations (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.80–1.04), although the test for subgroup difference was not significant (p = 0.18). **Conclusions.** In adults with T2DM, GLP-1 RAs are associated with a 14% relative reduction in cardiovascular mortality versus placebo, with consistent direction of effect across trials. Benefit appears most pronounced in patients with established CV disease, supporting current guideline recommendations to prioritize GLP-1 RAs in this population.

## 1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is one of the leading global drivers of premature cardiovascular (CV) mortality, accounting for an estimated 1.6 million CV deaths annually. The relative risk of CV death in adults with T2DM compared with non-diabetic peers is approximately 2-fold, even after adjusting for traditional risk factors. Glycemic control alone — as demonstrated in earlier intensive-glucose-lowering trials such as ACCORD and VADT — has produced inconsistent CV mortality signals, suggesting that drug class, beyond HbA1c lowering, mediates much of the CV outcome variation.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) are incretin-mimetic agents that augment glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppress glucagon, slow gastric emptying, and promote satiety. Beyond glycemic effects, they reduce systolic blood pressure modestly (1–3 mm Hg), lower body weight (3–6 kg), and produce favorable changes in lipoprotein profiles. Mechanistic studies in animal models and human surrogate-endpoint trials have suggested direct vascular effects, including reduced atherogenic plaque inflammation, improved endothelial function, and possibly reduced platelet aggregation.

Since 2016, multiple large cardiovascular outcome trials (CVOTs) — including the LEADER, SUSTAIN-6, REWIND, and AMPLITUDE-O trials — have demonstrated reductions in 3-point major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) for individual GLP-1 RA agents in patients with established CV disease or high CV risk. A 2019 individual-trial-level meta-analysis (Kristensen et al., Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol) reported a 12% reduction in MACE and a similar reduction in CV death, but was limited to seven trials and pre-dated the AMPLITUDE-O, oral semaglutide PIONEER-6, and several phase-3 weekly-dose trials that have since reported.

The contemporary GLP-1 RA literature now includes oral formulations, once-weekly subcutaneous regimens, and trials enrolling primary-prevention populations (i.e., T2DM patients with CV risk factors but without established CV disease). It is unclear whether the CV mortality benefit observed in established-CVD populations extends to primary prevention, whether the benefit is consistent across dosing schedules and routes of administration, and how robust the pooled effect is when contemporary trials are added to the prior evidence base.

We therefore conducted an updated systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of randomized, placebo-controlled trials of GLP-1 RAs in adults with T2DM. Our primary objective was to estimate the pooled effect of GLP-1 RA therapy on cardiovascular mortality. Secondary objectives were: (i) to characterize heterogeneity across trials and explore sources via pre-specified subgroup analyses; (ii) to assess the robustness of findings via leave-one-out, fixed-effect, and risk-of-bias-restricted sensitivity analyses; and (iii) to evaluate small-study effects and publication bias.

**Hypothesis.** We hypothesized a priori that GLP-1 RA therapy would reduce CV mortality by approximately 12–15% (pooled HR ~0.85–0.88) versus placebo in adults with T2DM, with a directionally larger but heterogeneity-compatible effect in established-CVD subgroups.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and registration

The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD42024-FIXTURE-0001) prior to formal screening. Reporting follows the PRISMA 2020 statement; the completed PRISMA checklist is provided in Supplement S1.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

We included randomized, placebo-controlled trials that:
- enrolled adults (≥18 years) with a clinical diagnosis of T2DM;
- assigned participants to a GLP-1 RA (any agent, any route, any dosing schedule) versus matched placebo;
- followed participants for ≥12 months;
- reported cardiovascular mortality as a pre-specified outcome (either as a component of MACE or independently adjudicated).

We excluded: trials enrolling type 1 diabetes or gestational diabetes; non-randomized or open-label studies; trials with active comparators (head-to-head GLP-1 RA vs other glucose-lowering drug classes); trials with follow-up <12 months; and conference abstracts without subsequent peer-reviewed publication.

### 2.3 Information sources and search strategy

We searched MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and ClinicalTrials.gov from January 1, 2010 through December 31, 2024. The search combined indexed terms and free-text keywords for ("GLP-1 receptor agonist" OR "liraglutide" OR "semaglutide" OR "dulaglutide" OR "exenatide" OR "lixisenatide" OR "albiglutide" OR "efpeglenatide") AND ("cardiovascular" OR "mortality" OR "death" OR "MACE") AND ("randomized" OR "trial"). Reference lists of included trials and prior systematic reviews were hand-searched. No language restriction was applied; non-English papers were translated as needed.

### 2.4 Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers (M.R.J., P.A.) independently screened titles and abstracts, then reviewed full texts. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or, if unresolved, by adjudication from a third reviewer (L.K.). For each included trial, we extracted: trial identifier, country/region, enrollment period, sample size, GLP-1 RA agent, dose and frequency, comparator, baseline mean age, sex distribution, mean HbA1c, proportion with established CV disease, primary outcome definition, follow-up duration, and event counts for CV death, all-cause mortality, and 3-point MACE.

### 2.5 Risk-of-bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed at the outcome level using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool across five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Each domain was rated low / some concerns / high. Two trials were rated "some concerns" due to incomplete blinding of outcome assessors; no trial was rated "high risk." Detailed domain-level ratings appear in Supplement S2.

### 2.6 Statistical analysis

The primary analysis pooled trial-level hazard ratios for cardiovascular mortality using a DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model. We chose random-effects a priori given the diversity in agent, dose, route, and population. Where trials reported only event counts (not HRs), we computed HRs using the Peto method.

Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q (with corresponding p value), I² (with 95% CI via the Higgins method), and τ² (between-trial variance). We considered I² <25% to indicate low heterogeneity, 25–50% moderate, 50–75% substantial, and >75% considerable.

**Pre-specified subgroup analyses** were:
1. Baseline CV disease status (established CV disease vs primary prevention);
2. Dosing schedule (daily injectable vs weekly injectable vs oral);
3. Route of administration (subcutaneous vs oral);
4. Trial duration (<3 years vs ≥3 years).

Subgroup differences were tested via interaction p values. We did not pre-specify a multiple-comparison correction given the small number of subgroups and exploratory intent.

**Pre-specified sensitivity analyses** were:
1. Leave-one-out (each trial removed in turn);
2. Fixed-effect (Mantel–Haenszel) model;
3. Restricted to trials at low risk of bias only;
4. Trim-and-fill adjustment for publication bias.

Small-study effects were assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically using Egger's regression test (significance threshold p < 0.10 given low power).

All analyses were performed in R version 4.3.2 using the `meta` (v6.5-0) and `metafor` (v4.4-0) packages. Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for the primary outcome.

### 2.7 Certainty of evidence

The certainty of the body of evidence for the primary outcome was rated using the GRADE framework, considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Study selection (PRISMA flow)

The database search returned 8,642 records (MEDLINE 3,114; Embase 4,201; CENTRAL 987; ClinicalTrials.gov 340). After removing 1,876 duplicates, 6,766 records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 6,612 were excluded as not relevant. We assessed 154 full-text reports for eligibility, excluding 142 (reasons: not randomized, n=24; non-T2DM population, n=18; active comparator only, n=31; follow-up <12 months, n=22; CV mortality not reported, n=29; duplicate publication, n=18). Twelve trials enrolling 87,432 unique patients were included in the quantitative synthesis.

### 3.2 Study characteristics

The 12 included trials enrolled between 1,733 and 9,901 participants (median 4,346) and were conducted across North America, Europe, Asia, and Latin America between 2014 and 2024. Median follow-up was 3.4 years (range 1.5–5.2 years). Eight trials evaluated subcutaneous weekly agents (semaglutide, dulaglutide, efpeglenatide, albiglutide), two evaluated daily injectables (liraglutide, lixisenatide), and two evaluated oral semaglutide. Mean baseline age ranged from 60.1 to 66.8 years, mean baseline HbA1c from 7.4% to 8.9%, and the proportion with established CV disease from 18% to 100%. Detailed per-trial characteristics appear in Supplement S3 (Table S3.1).

### 3.3 Risk of bias

Of the 12 trials, 10 were rated low risk of bias across all five RoB 2.0 domains. Two trials (Patel 2024, Yamamoto 2024) had "some concerns" due to deviations from intended interventions (open-label run-in periods). No trial was rated high risk of bias for the cardiovascular mortality outcome.

### 3.4 Primary outcome — cardiovascular mortality

Pooled across all 12 trials, GLP-1 RA therapy was associated with a statistically significant reduction in cardiovascular mortality compared with placebo (pooled HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.79–0.94, p < 0.001; Z = 3.42). Heterogeneity was low (I² = 18%, 95% CI 0%–52%; τ² = 0.012; Cochran's Q = 13.4, df = 11, p = 0.27). Per-trial estimates ranged from HR 0.75 (Andersson 2023) to HR 0.95 (Okafor 2021). The full forest plot is provided in `forest-plot-table.md` and Figure 1.

### 3.5 Secondary outcomes

All-cause mortality was reduced (pooled HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.83–0.96, p = 0.002; I² = 11%). Three-point MACE — the most commonly reported composite — was reduced (pooled HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.81–0.93, p < 0.001; I² = 22%). Non-fatal myocardial infarction (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.83–1.00, p = 0.04) and non-fatal stroke (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.75–0.94, p = 0.003) were also reduced. Heart-failure hospitalization showed a smaller, non-significant reduction (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.86–1.03, p = 0.18).

### 3.6 Subgroup analyses

GLP-1 RA therapy reduced CV mortality in trials enrolling participants with established CV disease (k=7, HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.74–0.93, I² = 22%) and showed a directionally consistent but non-significant effect in primary-prevention populations (k=5, HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.80–1.04, I² = 11%). The test for subgroup difference was not significant (interaction p = 0.18). By dosing schedule, daily (k=4, HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.74–0.97), weekly (k=6, HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.78–0.96), and oral (k=2, HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.72–1.10) regimens produced similar point estimates with overlapping confidence intervals.

### 3.7 Sensitivity analyses

Leave-one-out analysis produced pooled HRs ranging from 0.84 (when Andersson 2023 was excluded) to 0.88 (when Hernandez 2021 was excluded); statistical significance was retained in all 12 iterations. The fixed-effect (Mantel–Haenszel) model yielded HR 0.87 (95% CI 0.81–0.93), consistent with the random-effects estimate. Restricting to the 10 low-risk-of-bias trials produced HR 0.85 (95% CI 0.78–0.93). Trim-and-fill imputed one missing study; the adjusted pooled HR was 0.87 (95% CI 0.80–0.95).

### 3.8 Publication bias and small-study effects

Visual inspection of the funnel plot suggested mild asymmetry favoring positive results, with one notable outlier (Andersson 2023) in the lower-left quadrant. Egger's regression test was non-significant (intercept = -0.61, p = 0.21). Combined with the trim-and-fill result, there is little evidence of substantial publication bias affecting the primary estimate.

### 3.9 Certainty of evidence (GRADE)

The certainty of evidence for the primary outcome was rated **HIGH**. We did not downgrade for risk of bias (no high-risk trials), inconsistency (I² = 18%), indirectness (all trials directly addressed the PICO), imprecision (95% CI 0.79–0.94 excludes the null and the minimal important difference), or publication bias (Egger p = 0.21).

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Principal findings

In this updated meta-analysis of 12 randomized placebo-controlled trials enrolling 87,432 adults with type 2 diabetes, GLP-1 receptor agonists reduced cardiovascular mortality by 14% (pooled HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.79–0.94, p < 0.001). The effect was consistent across trials (I² = 18%) and robust across all pre-specified sensitivity analyses. All-cause mortality, 3-point MACE, non-fatal MI, and non-fatal stroke were also reduced; the heart-failure hospitalization effect was smaller and not statistically significant.

### 4.2 Comparison with prior literature

Our pooled HR of 0.86 is directionally consistent with — and quantitatively similar to — the 2019 Kristensen et al. meta-analysis (HR 0.88 for CV death, 7 trials), and with the 2021 ADA/EASD consensus that prioritized GLP-1 RAs for patients with established atherosclerotic CV disease. Adding five contemporary trials (Andersson 2023, Kowalski 2023, Patel 2024, Yamamoto 2024, plus the oral PIONEER-6 reanalysis) shifts the estimate marginally and tightens the confidence interval without changing the direction of effect.

### 4.3 Mechanistic considerations

Multiple plausible mechanisms underlie the observed CV mortality benefit. Direct effects on atherogenic plaque inflammation (mediated by GLP-1 receptors on macrophages and endothelial cells), modest reductions in systolic blood pressure (1–3 mm Hg) and body weight (3–6 kg), favorable shifts in apolipoprotein B / LDL particle number, and possibly reduced thrombotic risk via improved endothelial function have all been described in mechanistic studies. The relatively small heart-failure hospitalization effect — in contrast with the larger SGLT2-inhibitor heart-failure signal — suggests that the GLP-1 RA CV benefit may be predominantly atherothrombotic rather than hemodynamic.

### 4.4 Subgroup and clinical implications

The point estimate for established CV disease (HR 0.83) was directionally larger than for primary prevention (HR 0.91), but the interaction was not statistically significant. The primary-prevention subgroup is currently under-powered (k=5 trials) and the confidence interval (0.80–1.04) admits both meaningful benefit and no effect. We therefore cannot conclusively recommend GLP-1 RAs for CV mortality reduction in primary-prevention T2DM populations on the basis of this evidence, though the direction is encouraging and consistent with effects on intermediate outcomes.

### 4.5 Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. **First**, this is a trial-level meta-analysis; we did not have access to individual participant data, which would have permitted more granular subgroup analysis (e.g., by HbA1c at randomization or by glomerular filtration rate). **Second**, despite our broad search, publication bias cannot be ruled out, although the trim-and-fill and Egger's results are reassuring. **Third**, the included trials were predominantly conducted in high-income settings, which limits generalizability to low- and middle-income countries where T2DM burden is rapidly increasing. **Fourth**, two trials (Patel 2024, Yamamoto 2024) were rated "some concerns" for risk of bias; the low-risk-only sensitivity analysis showed an unchanged direction of effect, but residual bias from non-included trials cannot be ruled out. **Fifth**, follow-up duration (median 3.4 years) limits inference about long-term (>5-year) cardiovascular outcomes. **Sixth**, all included trials used placebo controls; the comparative effectiveness of GLP-1 RAs versus other glucose-lowering drug classes (notably SGLT2 inhibitors) requires head-to-head trials or network meta-analysis.

### 4.6 Conclusions

In adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus, GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with a 14% relative reduction in cardiovascular mortality versus placebo, with a high certainty of evidence (GRADE: HIGH). The benefit is most pronounced in patients with established CV disease, consistent with current guideline recommendations. The magnitude of effect in primary-prevention populations remains uncertain and warrants additional randomized evidence with extended follow-up. Future research should prioritize individual-participant-data meta-analyses, head-to-head comparisons with SGLT2 inhibitors, and trials in under-represented populations (low- and middle-income countries, advanced chronic kidney disease).
