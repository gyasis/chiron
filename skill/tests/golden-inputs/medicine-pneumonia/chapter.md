# Community-Acquired Pneumonia

> Teaching chapter — fixture content for Chiron medicine-domain pipeline.
> Drug doses below are standard adult outpatient/inpatient regimens; verify
> against current IDSA/ATS guidance before any clinical use.

## 1. Definition and Scope

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is an acute infection of the pulmonary
parenchyma acquired outside of a healthcare setting, in a patient who has not
been recently hospitalized (i.e., not within the prior 14 days) and does not
otherwise meet criteria for healthcare-associated pneumonia. CAP is
distinguished from hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) and ventilator-associated
pneumonia (VAP), which involve different microbiology and resistance patterns.

## 2. Epidemiology

- Incidence in adults in the United States is approximately 24 cases per 10,000 person-years, with substantially higher rates at the extremes of age (infants and adults ≥65 years).
- CAP is among the leading infectious causes of hospitalization and death in the United States.
- Estimated mortality varies sharply by severity: <1% in outpatients, 5–15% in hospitalized non-ICU patients, and up to 20–50% in patients requiring intensive care.
- Risk factors include age ≥65, current tobacco use, alcohol use disorder, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), congestive heart failure, diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, and prior splenectomy or asplenia.

## 3. Microbiology

CAP pathogens vary by host, severity, and geography. In the post-PCV13 era,
**Streptococcus pneumoniae** remains the most commonly identified bacterial
pathogen overall, but viral causes — particularly influenza A/B, respiratory
syncytial virus (RSV), and SARS-CoV-2 — are now identified at least as often
as any single bacterial cause when modern multiplex PCR is used.

### 3.1 Typical bacterial pathogens

- **Streptococcus pneumoniae** — most common identified bacterial cause; lobar consolidation classic
- **Haemophilus influenzae** — more common in COPD, smokers
- **Moraxella catarrhalis** — older adults with COPD
- **Staphylococcus aureus** — post-influenza superinfection; consider MRSA risk factors

### 3.2 Atypical bacterial pathogens

- **Mycoplasma pneumoniae** — younger adults, "walking pneumonia," extrapulmonary findings (hemolytic anemia, bullous myringitis)
- **Chlamydophila pneumoniae** — pharyngitis prodrome, hoarseness
- **Legionella pneumophila** — water exposure, hyponatremia, transaminitis, GI symptoms; severity often higher

### 3.3 Viral pathogens

- Influenza A and B
- Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)
- SARS-CoV-2
- Human metapneumovirus, parainfluenza, adenovirus, rhinovirus

### 3.4 Less common but high-stakes pathogens

- **Pseudomonas aeruginosa** — structural lung disease (bronchiectasis, severe COPD), recent broad-spectrum antibiotics
- **MRSA** — post-influenza, IV drug use, prior MRSA isolation, end-stage renal disease
- Endemic fungi (Coccidioides, Histoplasma, Blastomyces) by region

## 4. Pathophysiology

After inhalation or microaspiration, pathogens overcome upper-airway and
mucociliary defenses and reach the alveoli. Alveolar macrophages initiate
the local inflammatory response; cytokine release (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α) recruits
neutrophils. The alveoli fill with exudate (fibrin, neutrophils, edema fluid,
red cells), producing the classic four pathological stages of lobar pneumonia:
congestion, red hepatization, gray hepatization, and resolution. The resulting
**consolidation** is what is seen radiographically and felt as dullness to
percussion. Impaired gas exchange across consolidated alveoli leads to V/Q
mismatch and, in severe cases, frank hypoxemic respiratory failure.

## 5. Clinical Presentation

Classic symptoms include:

- Cough, productive of purulent or rusty sputum
- Fever, often with rigors
- Pleuritic chest pain
- Dyspnea
- Constitutional symptoms — malaise, fatigue, anorexia

Older adults may present atypically with confusion, falls, decompensation of a
chronic condition, or absence of fever. A normal temperature does not rule out
pneumonia in an elderly patient.

### 5.1 Physical examination

- Tachypnea (often the most sensitive vital-sign abnormality)
- Tachycardia, sometimes hypotension
- Hypoxemia
- Focal lung findings: dullness to percussion, increased tactile fremitus, bronchial breath sounds, egophony, crackles, occasional pleural rub

## 6. Diagnostic Workup

### 6.1 Clinical decision rules for severity

- **CURB-65** — one point each for: Confusion, Urea (BUN >19 mg/dL or >7 mmol/L), Respiratory rate ≥30, Blood pressure (SBP <90 or DBP ≤60), age ≥65. Score 0–1: outpatient; 2: consider admission; ≥3: admit, consider ICU.
- **PSI / PORT score** — more granular, validates outpatient triage in 5 risk classes.
- IDSA/ATS minor and major criteria help identify patients needing **ICU-level care**, including septic shock requiring vasopressors and respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation.

### 6.2 Imaging

- **Chest radiograph (PA and lateral)** is required for diagnosis in most settings; CAP cannot be reliably diagnosed by exam alone.
- Findings: lobar consolidation, air bronchograms, interstitial infiltrates, parapneumonic effusion.
- **CT chest** is reserved for diagnostic uncertainty, suspected complication (cavitation, abscess, empyema), or when the chest radiograph is non-diagnostic in a clinically convincing case.

### 6.3 Microbiologic testing (severity-tailored per IDSA/ATS 2019)

| Test | Outpatient | Inpatient non-ICU | ICU |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blood cultures (×2) | Not routine | Selectively (e.g., severe, MRSA risk) | Yes |
| Sputum Gram stain + culture | Not routine | Selectively | Yes if able |
| Pneumococcal urinary antigen | Not routine | Selectively | Yes |
| Legionella urinary antigen | Not routine | Selectively | Yes |
| Influenza PCR (in season) | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| SARS-CoV-2 testing | Yes | Yes | Yes |

### 6.4 Biomarkers

- **Procalcitonin (PCT)** — should NOT be used to withhold antibiotics at the time of CAP diagnosis. It may help guide duration in some settings; clinical judgment remains primary.
- CRP and WBC are non-specific but support the clinical impression.

## 7. Treatment

### 7.1 Empiric outpatient therapy (no comorbidities, no recent antibiotics)

- **Amoxicillin** 1 g PO three times daily, **OR**
- **Doxycycline** 100 mg PO twice daily, **OR**
- A macrolide (**azithromycin** 500 mg PO on day 1, then 250 mg PO daily for 4 days), **only where local pneumococcal macrolide resistance is <25%**.

### 7.2 Empiric outpatient therapy (with comorbidities — chronic heart, lung, liver, renal disease; diabetes; alcohol use; malignancy; asplenia)

- A **respiratory fluoroquinolone** (levofloxacin 750 mg PO daily, or moxifloxacin 400 mg PO daily), **OR**
- A **beta-lactam** (amoxicillin/clavulanate 875/125 mg PO twice daily, or cefuroxime 500 mg PO twice daily) **plus** a **macrolide** (azithromycin) **or doxycycline**.

### 7.3 Empiric inpatient therapy — non-severe (non-ICU)

- A **beta-lactam** (ceftriaxone 1–2 g IV daily, ampicillin/sulbactam 1.5–3 g IV every 6 hours, or cefotaxime) **plus** a **macrolide** (azithromycin 500 mg IV/PO daily), **OR**
- A **respiratory fluoroquinolone** monotherapy (levofloxacin 750 mg IV/PO daily, or moxifloxacin 400 mg IV/PO daily).

### 7.4 Empiric inpatient therapy — severe (ICU)

- A **beta-lactam** (ceftriaxone, ampicillin/sulbactam, or cefotaxime) **plus** either a **macrolide** OR a **respiratory fluoroquinolone**.
- Macrolide-containing regimens have observational data suggesting a mortality benefit in severe CAP (likely related to pneumococcal coverage redundancy and immunomodulatory effects).

### 7.5 When to add MRSA or Pseudomonas coverage

Add empiric MRSA or *Pseudomonas* coverage **only if locally validated risk factors are present**, such as:

- Prior respiratory isolation of MRSA or *Pseudomonas* (within the past year)
- Recent hospitalization AND parenteral antibiotics (within the past 90 days), with locally validated risk factors

If empiric coverage is started, **de-escalate within 48 hours** if cultures and nasal MRSA PCR (high negative predictive value) do not support continuation.

- **MRSA**: vancomycin (with target trough/AUC) OR linezolid 600 mg IV/PO every 12 hours
- **Pseudomonas**: piperacillin/tazobactam, cefepime, meropenem, or levofloxacin (if not already used)

### 7.6 Adjunctive therapy

- **Oseltamivir** for confirmed or strongly suspected influenza, regardless of duration of symptoms in hospitalized patients.
- **Glucocorticoids** are NOT routinely recommended in non-severe CAP. Use is appropriate in patients with refractory septic shock or other steroid indications (e.g., COPD exacerbation).

### 7.7 Duration

- **Uncomplicated CAP**: a minimum of **5 days** of antibiotics, provided the patient is afebrile for 48–72 hours, hemodynamically stable, eating, and has no more than one CAP-related vital-sign abnormality at the time of stopping.
- **Legionella, MRSA, *Pseudomonas***, or complicated disease (cavitation, empyema, extrapulmonary spread): longer courses, typically 7–14 days or guided by source control.

## 8. Complications

- **Parapneumonic effusion** — sympathetic pleural fluid; sample if >1 cm on lateral decubitus.
- **Empyema** — frank pus or organisms on Gram stain in pleural fluid; requires drainage.
- **Lung abscess** — cavitating consolidation; consider anaerobes, *S. aureus*.
- **Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)** — bilateral infiltrates with refractory hypoxemia.
- **Sepsis and septic shock** — manage per surviving sepsis guidelines.
- **Metastatic infection** — meningitis, endocarditis (especially with *S. pneumoniae* and *S. aureus*).
- **Post-pneumonia decompensation** — incident heart failure, MI, and stroke risk are elevated for weeks after CAP.

## 9. Prevention

### 9.1 Pneumococcal vaccines (adults)

Per current ACIP guidance, adults aged ≥65 (and younger adults with qualifying
conditions) should receive pneumococcal vaccination. Acceptable approaches
include:

- **PCV20** alone, **OR**
- **PCV15** followed by **PPSV23** ≥1 year later (or ≥8 weeks for immunocompromised).

PCV13 remains in use in some pediatric and adult settings; the agent should
align with current ACIP recommendations at time of vaccination.

### 9.2 Influenza vaccine

Annual seasonal influenza vaccination is recommended for all adults ≥6 months
without contraindication. Influenza vaccination reduces both influenza
pneumonia and post-influenza bacterial superinfection.

### 9.3 Other measures

- Smoking cessation
- COVID-19 vaccination per current guidance
- RSV vaccination per current ACIP recommendations for older adults and during pregnancy

## 10. High-Yield Clinical Pearls

1. **Procalcitonin should not be used to withhold antibiotics in suspected CAP.** It may help shorten duration, but the diagnosis and decision to treat are clinical and radiographic.
2. **Hyponatremia + transaminitis + GI symptoms in a smoker with severe pneumonia** should raise immediate suspicion for *Legionella*. Send urinary antigen.
3. **Older adults with CAP often present without fever.** Confusion and falls may be the only finding; have a low threshold for chest imaging.
4. **Nasal MRSA PCR has high negative predictive value (~95–99%) for MRSA pneumonia.** A negative result is a strong signal to de-escalate empiric vancomycin/linezolid.
5. **Repeat chest imaging is not required** for clinically improving CAP unless the patient is at high risk for underlying malignancy (e.g., older smoker), in which case follow-up imaging at 6–12 weeks is appropriate.
6. **Treatment failure at 72 hours** should prompt re-evaluation: wrong bug (resistant pneumococcus, *Legionella*, MRSA, *Pseudomonas*, fungal, TB), wrong diagnosis (PE, heart failure, malignancy, organizing pneumonia), or a complication (empyema, abscess).

## 11. Summary

CAP is a clinical syndrome with a heterogeneous microbiology that tracks with
host risk factors and disease severity. Diagnosis is clinical plus
radiographic. Severity assessment (CURB-65, PSI, IDSA/ATS criteria) guides
the site of care. Empiric therapy is selected by setting and risk factors,
with broader coverage reserved for ICU-level disease and patients with
locally validated MRSA or *Pseudomonas* risk. Most uncomplicated cases
respond to 5–7 days of appropriately chosen antibiotics. Vaccination,
smoking cessation, and influenza prevention remain the cornerstones of
primary prevention.
