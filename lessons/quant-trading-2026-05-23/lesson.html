Chapter 1

The four parts of every price series

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
  • Decompose any price series into trend, seasonality, cycle, and noise.
  • Identify which component a trading strategy is exploiting.
  • Test whether a residual is true cyclicality or fake (overfit to noise).
⚙ Technical Requirements
  • A Python environment with pandas, numpy, statsmodels.
  • Historical OHLCV data for SPY (any source — Yahoo Finance, Polygon, or AlphaVantage).
  • Optional: Jupyter notebook for inline plots.

Every price chart decomposes into the same four pieces. Knowing which one you're trading separates a real edge from a story you're telling yourself.

Decomposition

ComponentWhat it isHow to testTradeable?
TrendLong-run directionLinear regression of price vs time; slope significanceYes — trend-following
SeasonalityPeriodic (day-of-week, hour-of-day)First differencing + ACF at known periodsYes, but arbitraged in liquid markets
CycleMulti-month wave, non-calendricalSpectral analysis; usually weak signalSometimes — regime detection
NoiseResidual; should be i.i.d.Heavy-tailed in practice; vol clusteringNever directly
ℹNote

i.i.d. = "independent and identically distributed." A textbook assumption that financial returns rarely satisfy. Heavy tails, autocorrelation, and volatility clustering all violate i.i.d., which is why robust strategies don't rely on Gaussian assumptions.

Worked example — SPY decomposition

The 20-day MA (blue) tracks the underlying trend; price (orange) wobbles around it. Subtract the MA → you isolate the cycle + noise components. If the residual passes a Ljung-Box autocorrelation test, you've isolated tradeable cyclicality. Most of the time it doesn't.

✎Hands-on Exercise

Compute your own decomposition. Load 60 days of SPY, apply statsmodels.tsa.seasonal.STL, and plot the four components. Compare your residual's autocorrelation at lag 1 to a random series. If they look the same, your residual is noise.

💡Tip

If your backtest depends on a pattern you can't classify as one of the four components, you're overfitting to noise. The discipline is in the labelling, not the backtest.

Summary

In this chapter you learned:

  • Every price series = trend + seasonality + cycle + noise.
  • Each component has a distinct statistical test.
  • Tradeable: trend (yes), seasonality (rare in liquid markets), cycle (sometimes), noise (never).
  • Overfit-to-noise is the #1 killer of retail strategies.

Chapter 2

Mean reversion — the pairs trade

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
  • Define and test for cointegration between two assets.
  • Construct a rolling z-score from a log-spread.
  • Apply entry / exit / stop rules for a pairs trade.
  • Recognise the four classic ways mean-reversion strategies fail in production.

When two co-moving assets diverge, the spread between them tends to snap back toward its mean. Simplest, oldest, most-arbitraged edge in systematic trading.

The math

\[ S_t = \log(P^A_t / P^B_t) \quad\quad z_t = \frac{S_t - \mu}{\sigma} \]
Spread of a cointegrated pair (top); rolling z-score with window μ, σ (bottom). Typical window: 60–90 days.

Trade lifecycle

PYTHON · pairs-trade pseudocode
# 1. Cointegrate
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import coint
score, pvalue, _ = coint(prices_A, prices_B)
if pvalue > 0.05: return  # not cointegrated → skip

# 2. Z-normalise
spread = np.log(prices_A / prices_B)
mu, sigma = spread.rolling(60).mean(), spread.rolling(60).std()
z = (spread - mu) / sigma

# 3. Enter — short spread at z > +2σ, long at z < −2σ
position = 0
if z[-1] > 2:  position = -1
elif z[-1] < -2: position = +1

# 4. Exit — target at z = 0, stop at |z| > 3.5
if abs(z[-1]) > 3.5 or sign(z[-1]) != sign(z[entry]):
    close_position()
⚠Warning

Cointegration is necessary but not sufficient. Two assets can pass an Engle-Granger test on historical data and still decouple tomorrow. The math tells you when the relationship was true; it doesn't promise when it stops.

Live spread

Bands at ±2σ are entry triggers; ±3.5σ is the stop. Look at sessions 15–22 — a clean short-the-spread setup. Session 38 hits +3.5σ — a stop-out.

Four ways mean reversion dies

#1
Regime change — pair stops cointegrating. Spread runs and doesn't return. Symptom: stop-out at |z|>3.5σ every entry.
#2
Crowded trade — everyone reads the same paper. Edge arbitraged away. Symptom: declining Sharpe over rolling 6mo.
#3
Liquidity gap — one leg can't be exited at marketable size mid-cycle. Symptom: slippage 3× expected.
#4
Funding shock — margin spikes, forced close at max DD. Symptom: max DD coincides with vol spike, not pair fundamentals.
★Best Practice

Re-test cointegration monthly on a rolling window. If the p-value crosses 0.05, halt new entries immediately and exit existing positions on the next mean-touch.

Summary

In this chapter you learned:

  • Pairs trading exploits cointegration — a stationary linear combination of two assets.
  • Z-score normalises the spread; entry at ±2σ, exit at 0, stop at ±3.5σ.
  • Cointegration is necessary but not sufficient — re-test monthly.
  • Four classic failure modes: regime change, crowded trade, liquidity gap, funding shock.

Chapter 3

Risk-adjusted return — the Sharpe ratio

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
  • Compute and annualise a Sharpe ratio.
  • Interpret Sharpe across the noise / retail / professional / rare / suspicious tiers.
  • Recognise when headline returns mislead.

Returns alone don't measure skill. A 30% return at 50% volatility is the same Sharpe as a 6% return at 10% volatility. One will scare you out of the trade; the other won't. Sharpe is the dimensionless number that survives across timeframes.

Formula + annualisation

\[ SR = \frac{E[R_p] - R_f}{\sigma_p} \quad\quad SR_{ann} = SR_{daily} \cdot \sqrt{252} \]
Sharpe (top). Annualised from daily (bottom) — 252 trading days/year.

Reading the Sharpe

Annualised SharpeTierWhat it means
< 0.5NoiseYou'd do better in a Treasury bill
0.5 – 1.0Retail-gradeJust beats noise
1.0 – 2.0ProfessionalSustainable, manageable drawdowns
2.0 – 3.0RareTop-decile fund
> 3.0SuspiciousLikely overfit, lookahead bias, or misspecified σ

Worked example — two strategies, different Sharpe

Strategy A — low headline return
SR = (12% − 4%) / 8%
Sharpe = 1.00
WINNER
Strategy B — high headline return
SR = (25% − 4%) / 25%
Sharpe = 0.84
LOSER
Strategy A has the HIGHER Sharpe despite lower headline returns. This is exactly why Sharpe matters — headline returns are the trap.
⚠Warning

If your Sharpe is greater than 3 and you didn't intentionally engineer survivorship bias, look for it anyway. Sharpe > 3 in real strategies is rare enough that it usually indicates a methodological error.

💡Tip

Always compute the rolling 90-day Sharpe in addition to the lifetime number. A single 5-year Sharpe of 1.4 hides whether the strategy stopped working in the last quarter. Rolling Sharpe surfaces regime change.

Summary

In this chapter you learned:

  • Sharpe = (return − risk-free) / vol. Annualise daily by √252.
  • >1 professional, >2 rare, >3 suspicious.
  • Same Sharpe across scales — it's the only metric that survives the unit change.
  • Always pair lifetime Sharpe with rolling 90-day to spot regime change.

Chapter 4

Position sizing — Kelly and half-Kelly

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
  • Apply the Kelly criterion to size positions given win-rate p and payoff b.
  • Compare full Kelly, half Kelly, and quarter Kelly on growth vs drawdown.
  • Understand why every professional shrinks Kelly in production.

How much to risk per trade is the second-biggest decision in trading (after when to exit). Kelly gives the math; fractional Kelly survives production.

The formula

\[ f^* = \frac{p \cdot b - q}{b} = \frac{p(b+1) - 1}{b} \quad\quad q = 1 - p \]
Growth-optimal fraction of capital. p = win probability, b = payoff ratio (risk $1 to win $b).

Sizing decision tree

Why full Kelly is dangerous

Full KellyHalf KellyQuarter Kelly
Long-run growth rate100%~75%~44%
Typical max DD40–60%20–30%10–15%
Survives parameter error?NoYes (small)Yes (large)
Industry usageRareStandardConservative
✎Hands-on Exercise

Compute Kelly for your last backtest. Say it yielded p = 0.55, b = 1.2 (risk $1 to win $1.20). Plug into the formula:

f* = (0.55 × 1.2 − 0.45) / 1.2 = 0.175

Full Kelly says size at 17.5% per trade. Half Kelly: 8.75%. Now ask yourself — at half Kelly, can you stomach a 25% drawdown? If not, drop to quarter Kelly. The maths is the upper bound. Your psychology sets the actual size.

⚠Warning

Full Kelly assumes you know p and b exactly. In production they are estimates with error bars. Even a small overestimate of p compounds catastrophically. Half Kelly trades 25% of theoretical growth for 50% drawdown reduction — that's the cheap insurance the maths doesn't tell you to buy.

Summary

In this chapter you learned:

  • Kelly criterion gives the growth-optimal capital fraction f*.
  • Half Kelly is industry standard — sacrifices 25% of growth for 50% drawdown reduction.
  • Hard-cap any single position at 25% of capital regardless of Kelly.
  • Your psychology sets the actual size — Kelly is the upper bound.

Chapter 5

Risk management — stops, trailing, drawdown budgets

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
  • Set entry stops BEFORE the trade and never widen in-trade.
  • Apply trailing-stop discipline (UP only, in a long).
  • Define a per-portfolio drawdown budget and a halt-trading trigger.
  • Recognise the discretionary-override-of-quant-rule failure mode.

Position sizing answers how much; the stop-loss protocol answers when to exit when wrong. The exit is where positive-expectancy strategies become flat or losing.

The protocol

  1. Set stop level BEFORE entering. Never widen in-trade.
  2. Trail stop UP only (in a long position).
  3. At the stop: close immediately, no second-guessing.
  4. At the target: close, book, do not chase the next leg.
  5. Halt trading when portfolio drawdown exceeds budget.

Drawdown budgets — by trader profile

ProfilePer-trade stopStrategy DD haltPortfolio DD halt
Discretionary swing1–2% of capital—10%
Systematic single-strategyPer backtest σ2× backtest max DD—
Multi-strategy fundPer book/strategyPer stop-loss policy10–15% (mandate)
Quant retailPer Kelly fraction1.5× backtest max DD20%

A case — when the stop gets argued with

✎Hands-on Exercise

Setup: AAPL/MSFT pairs strategy, $100k capital, half-Kelly. Backtest Sharpe 1.4, max DD 12%.

Month 4: z hits +3.8σ. Rule says stop at |z|>3.5. AAPL beat earnings, MSFT missed. You think: "fundamentals-driven, this MUST mean-revert when MSFT recovers." You hold.

Month 7: z at +5σ. Down 14% — past your 12% DD budget. You think: "too late to stop now." You hold.

Month 9: z settles at +6.2σ. Cointegration permanently broken (services-revenue mix vs cloud growth diverged). You close. Total loss: 22% of capital.

Diagnosis: the single most costly decision was overriding the stop at Month 4. Every loss after that is downstream. The rule existed because backtest data said |z|>3.5 historically signalled regime change. A fundamentals story is not a reason to override a quant rule — it's the classic discretionary-override failure mode.

⚠Warning

If you find yourself negotiating with a position about whether to honour the stop, the stop was right and you are wrong. Close it. Document the trade in your journal. Move on.

Summary

In this chapter you learned:

  • Stops are pre-paid losses. Honour them mechanically.
  • Trail UP only — never down. Widening a stop is renaming a trade as an investment.
  • Define per-profile drawdown budgets and a portfolio halt trigger.
  • The most expensive single mistake is overriding a quant rule with a discretionary story.

Chapter 6

Cheat sheet — pick the right tool

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
  • Match a market situation to the appropriate strategy family.
  • Internalise the five non-negotiable rules of disciplined trading.

Strategy ↔ situation matrix

SituationStrategy familyKey metricBiggest risk
Persistent trend + low volTrend-following / momentumSharpe + max DD ratioRegime change (whipsaws)
Co-moving assets divergePairs / cointegrationSpread z-scoreCointegration breaks permanently
Periodic anomalySeasonality / calendarOut-of-sample SharpeArbitraged away in liquid markets
Multi-leg arbitrageStat arb / market makingP&L per share + inventory turnLiquidity shock; latency loss
Convex payoff structuresOptions / vol sellingSortino, tail VaRVol-of-vol blow-up
None of the aboveStay in cash—Boredom-driven entry

The five rules (memorise)

  • Sharpe over returns. Headline returns lie; Sharpe normalises.
  • Half Kelly, never full. Edge estimates always shrink in production.
  • Set the stop before entering. Never widen in-trade.
  • Cointegration is necessary, not sufficient. Validate out-of-sample.
  • If you can't classify the edge as trend / season / cycle / arb, you're overfitting noise.
★Best Practice

Print this page. Pin it above your trading desk. Re-read it on day one of every new strategy and on day one of every drawdown.
