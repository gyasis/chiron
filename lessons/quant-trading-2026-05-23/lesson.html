Chapter 1 of 5

Why quant — and the four parts of every price chart

Markets are statistically hostile. Without quantitative discipline, every trade becomes a narrative — and narratives generate positions that survive past their statistical justification. This chapter introduces the mathematical guardrails and then immediately puts them to work: every price series decomposes into trend, seasonality, cycle, and noise. Spotting which one you're trading is the difference between an edge and a story.

Why you careQuant trading lives at the intersection of probability, optimisation, and discipline. The math is the safety net — once you can read it, you stop trusting screenshots and start trusting backtests.

Glossary

alpha
Excess return above a benchmark — the portion of return NOT explained by market exposure.
beta
Sensitivity of a position to the market — beta=1 moves with the market 1:1.
sharpe
Risk-adjusted return: (return - risk-free rate) / standard deviation. >1 is professional, >2 is rare.
mean reversion
The tendency of a series to drift back toward its mean. Strategies bet on snap-back after extreme moves.

Anatomy of a time series

1
Trend

Long-run direction. Up, down, sideways. Linear regression of price vs time gives slope.

2
Seasonality

Periodic component — day-of-week, intraday hour. Removed with first differencing or detrending.

3
Cycle

Multi-month wave that isn't calendrical. Hard to model — usually treated as residual.

4
Noise

Everything left. Ideally i.i.d.; rarely is. Heavy tails, autocorrelation, vol clustering.

Chapter 2 of 5

Mean reversion — the pairs trade

When two co-moving assets diverge, the spread between them often snaps back. This chapter walks the cointegrate → z-normalise → enter → exit protocol with a worked example. Then it shows the four ways the strategy actually dies in production: regime change, crowded trade, liquidity gap, funding shock.

\[ z_t = \frac{S_t - \mu}{\sigma}, \quad \text{where } S_t = \log(P^A_t / P^B_t) \]

Mean-reversion trade lifecycle

1
Cointegrate

Test that the pair's spread is stationary. Engle-Granger or Johansen.

2
Z-normalise

Standardise the spread to a z-score (rolling-window μ, σ).

3
Enter

Short the spread when z > +2; long when z < −2.

4
Exit

Close at z = 0 (target) or stop at |z| > 3.5 (regime break).

How mean reversion dies

#1

Regime change

Pair stops cointegrating. The spread takes off and never returns.

Symptom: stop-out at |z|>3.5 every entry
#2

Crowded trade

Everyone reads the same paper. Edge disappears as alpha gets arbitraged.

Symptom: declining Sharpe over rolling 6mo
#3

Liquidity gap

Half-cycle into the trade, one leg can't be exited at marketable size.

Symptom: slippage > expected by 3x
#4

Funding shock

Margin requirement spikes. Forced to close at maximum drawdown.

Symptom: max DD coincides with vol spike
Chapter 3 of 5

Risk-adjusted return — the Sharpe ratio

Returns alone don't measure skill. The Sharpe ratio normalises return by volatility, so a 30% return at 50% vol becomes comparable to a 6% return at 10% vol. This chapter derives Sharpe in four steps, shows it rolling over a live strategy, and compares four backtested approaches by their Sharpe alone.

Why you careSharpe is the only number that survives across timeframes. A 30% return with 50% vol is the same Sharpe as a 6% return with 10% vol — but one will scare you out of the trade and the other won't.
\[ SR = \frac{E[R_p] - R_f}{\sigma_p} \quad \text{annualised: } SR_{ann} = SR_{daily}\,\sqrt{252} \]

Deriving the Sharpe ratio

1
Excess return

Subtract the risk-free rate from your strategy's return.

2
Standardise

Divide by the standard deviation of returns. Same return at half the vol = double the Sharpe.

3
Annualise

Multiply daily Sharpe by √252 (252 trading days/year).

4
Compare

Sharpe >1 is professional; >2 is rare; >3 is suspicious.

Chapter 4 of 5

Position sizing — Kelly and the half-Kelly heuristic

How much to risk per trade is the second-biggest decision in trading. The Kelly criterion gives a closed-form answer — but full Kelly is dangerous in practice because it assumes your edge estimate is perfect. This chapter walks the math, then explains why every professional shrinks Kelly to half or quarter.

\[ f^* = \frac{p \cdot b - q}{b} = \frac{p(b+1) - 1}{b} \]

Position sizing protocol

1
Estimate edge

Win rate p, payoff ratio b. Use BACKTEST data, not a guess.

2
Apply Kelly

f* = (p·b − q) / b. Compute your ideal fraction of capital.

3
Discount

Multiply by 0.5 (half Kelly) or 0.25 (quarter Kelly) for safety.

4
Cap

Hard cap at 25% of capital per position regardless. Tail risk.

Full Kelly vs fractional Kelly
Full Kelly — Mathematically growth-optimal but VOLATILE. Drawdowns of 40-60% are not edge cases. Assumes your edge estimate is perfect.
Fractional Kelly (½) — Sacrifices ~25% of growth for half the drawdown. Industry standard — accounts for parameter uncertainty.
Your backtest shows p=0.55, b=1.2 (you risk $1 to win $1.20). Full Kelly says f* ≈ 0.17. Why would a professional NOT size at 17%?
  1. Right — parameter uncertainty is the killer. Half-Kelly trades 25% growth for 50% drawdown reduction.
  2. Backwards — Full Kelly is aggressive, not conservative. Institutions size BELOW it.
  3. Kelly works for any edge-with-known-payoff. Trading just adds noise to the parameter estimates.
  4. No — Kelly is a fraction of available capital. The math works at any wealth level.
Chapter 5 of 5

Risk management — stops, trailing, and drawdown budgets

Position sizing answers 'how much,' but the stop-loss protocol answers 'when to exit when wrong.' The exit is where amateurs lose money. This final chapter walks the stop-protocol flow live, with a BTC candlestick chart showing the kind of volatility the protocol exists to survive.

Glossary

drawdown
Peak-to-trough decline of a portfolio. Max drawdown is the deepest such decline over a period.
volatility
Standard deviation of returns, usually annualised. Higher vol = wider range of outcomes = more position-sizing discipline required.
risk budget
How much you're willing to lose per position before exiting. Set BEFORE entry, never adjusted in-trade.
