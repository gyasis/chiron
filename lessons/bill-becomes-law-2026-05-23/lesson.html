Chapter 1

Birth of a bill — drafting and introduction

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
  • Distinguish a bill from joint, concurrent, and simple resolutions.
  • Explain how Congressional bill numbers (H.R. vs S.) are assigned.
  • Identify who actually writes the text of a federal bill.
  • Trace the full path a bill follows from introduction to law.
⚙ Technical Requirements
  • A free account at congress.gov to track a real bill.
  • Familiarity with U.S. Constitution Article I (legislative powers).

The Constitution gives "all legislative powers herein granted" to Congress (Article I §1). What it does NOT say is who may introduce a bill. By two centuries of tradition, the answer is: only a sitting member of the chamber where the bill originates.

Bills vs resolutions

Not every document Congress passes becomes law. Four forms exist, and only two ever land on the President's desk:

FormDesignationBecomes law?Purpose
BillH.R. # / S. #YesMost legislation
Joint resolutionH.J. Res. # / S.J. Res. #Yes (mostly)Continuing appropriations, war powers, proposed constitutional amendments (these go directly to the states, not the President)
Concurrent resolutionH. Con. Res. # / S. Con. Res. #NoBi-chamber statements; budget framework
Simple resolutionH. Res. # / S. Res. #NoInternal chamber rules; one-chamber statements
ℹNote

A joint resolution proposing a constitutional amendment is the one exception that bypasses the President entirely. Once both chambers pass it by 2/3 majority, it goes directly to the states for ratification.

Who drafts the text

Members rarely write the text themselves. Real authorship usually involves:

  • Member personal staff — initial outline, political framing.
  • Office of Legislative Counsel — House and Senate each maintain a non-partisan attorney office that translates policy intent into statutory language. This is the technical drafting step.
  • Lobbyists, executive agencies, outside policy shops — frequently supply working drafts the member then sponsors.
  • Government Publishing Office — handles typesetting and version control once introduced.
✎Hands-on Exercise

Pick any recent bill at congress.gov/bill. Click "Text" → "View Text Comparison." You will see multiple versions of the same bill — introduced, reported in committee, engrossed (passed by originating chamber), received (in the other chamber), enrolled (final agreed text). This is the same artifact mutating as it moves through the gauntlet.

Sponsorship and co-sponsors

Every bill has exactly one sponsor (the member who introduces it). Other members may sign on as co-sponsors. Co-sponsors signal political support but have no procedural power.

💡Tip

Co-sponsor counts are a useful but noisy proxy for momentum. A high count plus committee chair support is the strongest predictor of forward motion. A high count without chair support usually means the bill dies in committee with a long signatory list.

Introduction and numbering

  1. House: sponsor drops a copy in the hopper (a wooden box on the Clerk's desk).
  2. Senate: sponsor rises during morning hour and addresses the presiding officer.
  3. Numbering: Clerk assigns the next sequential number prefixed with the chamber (H.R. 1, H.R. 2 in the House; S. 1, S. 2 in the Senate). Numbers reset every Congress (a 2-year cycle).
  4. First reading: title read into the record.
  5. Referral: chamber parliamentarian assigns the bill to one or more committees based on jurisdiction.

Chapter 2

Committee stage — where most bills die

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
  • Explain why ~90% of bills die in committee.
  • Identify the four types of Congressional committees.
  • Describe a markup session: what amendments can be offered and how votes are tallied.
  • Use a discharge petition to bypass a hostile committee chair.

If a bill ever became a "how a bill becomes a law" cliche, the committee stage is the bottleneck the cliche skips. This is where most bills die. Understanding why is the single most important lesson in legislative process.

Why committees exist

Congress has 535 members and tens of thousands of bills per session. No member can read every bill. The committee system is the division of labor that makes legislating tractable: each committee specializes in a policy domain (Judiciary, Armed Services, Ways and Means, Energy and Commerce) and does the deep technical work on bills referred to it.

⚠Warning

Committee chairs are powerful. The chair controls the agenda. If the chair refuses to schedule a hearing, the bill is effectively dead — there is no countdown, no automatic discharge, no procedural escape unless the full chamber forces it (see Discharge Petition below).

Types of committees

TypeLifespanPurposeExample
StandingPermanentWorkhorse — drafts and reports billsHouse Judiciary, Senate Finance
Select / SpecialTime-limitedInvestigation or focused studyHouse January 6th Select Committee
JointPermanentBoth chambers; usually study-onlyJoint Economic Committee
ConferenceOne-billReconcile House and Senate versionsAd-hoc per bill

Subcommittee work

Most committees split into subcommittees with narrower jurisdiction. A bill is typically referred from full committee down to the appropriate subcommittee, which holds hearings (witnesses, expert testimony) and then either reports the bill back to full committee or lets it die.

Markup and reporting

A markup is the working session where the committee literally marks up the bill — proposes amendments, debates, votes, and decides whether to advance.

  1. Amendments offered — any committee member may propose. Amendments debated and voted on individually.
  2. Amendment in the nature of a substitute — a rewrite of the entire bill. Common tactic to clean up or radically reshape.
  3. Final vote — committee votes on the (now-amended) bill: report favorably, unfavorably, or without recommendation.
  4. Committee report — formal written document explaining the bill, amendments, and dissenting views. Becomes part of the legislative history courts rely on.
★Best Practice

Legislative history matters. If you ever read a Supreme Court opinion that interprets statutory language, you will see citations to the committee report. Courts use committee reports to determine Congressional intent when text is ambiguous. Write the committee report carefully if you ever staff one.

Discharge petition (escape hatch)

What if the committee chair refuses to act? The House has a rarely-used procedure: the discharge petition. If 218 members (a House majority) sign, the bill is "discharged" from committee and goes directly to the floor.

ℹNote

Discharge petitions are extremely rare. The leadership of both parties historically discourage them — they break the unwritten committee-respect rules. About one bill per Congress actually clears the discharge threshold. The Senate has no direct equivalent (Rule XIV and unanimous-consent workarounds exist).

Summary

In this chapter you learned:

  • Most bills die because the committee chair never schedules a hearing.
  • Four committee types: standing (workhorse), select (focused), joint (both chambers), conference (reconciliation).
  • Markup = the working session where amendments are voted on and the bill is reported.
  • Committee reports become legislative history; courts use them to interpret intent.
  • Discharge petition is the House escape hatch — 218 signatures — but rarely succeeds.

Chapter 3

Floor consideration — debate, amendment, vote

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
  • Compare House and Senate floor procedure.
  • Explain how the House Rules Committee shapes debate and amendment.
  • Define the filibuster and how cloture (60 votes) ends it.
  • Identify voice, division, roll call, and electronic vote tally types.

The two chambers run their floors very differently. The House is majoritarian and tightly controlled by its Rules Committee. The Senate is consensus-driven and gives individual senators enormous leverage via the filibuster.

House vs Senate procedure

Floor procedure side-by-side
HOUSE (435 members)

Rules Committee dictates debate
WARN
SENATE (100 members)
Unlimited debate unless 60 vote for cloture

WARN
Same bill text, two very different gauntlets. A bill that sails through the House may die on a Senate filibuster — or vice versa.

House Rules Committee

The Rules Committee writes a rule for each bill specifying: (a) total debate time, (b) which amendments may be offered. Three rule types:

Rule typeAmendments allowedTypical use
OpenAny germane amendmentAppropriations; low-stakes bills
ClosedNoneLeadership priorities; tax bills
Structured / modifiedOnly pre-listed amendmentsMost major legislation today
💡Tip

The shift to closed and structured rules over the last 30 years is the single biggest change in how the House operates. In the 1970s, ~85% of rules were open; today, less than 5% are open.

Senate filibuster + cloture

Any senator may speak indefinitely on any matter (with rare exceptions like budget reconciliation). To end debate against the speaker will requires cloture — a successful motion under Rule XXII, requiring 60 votes.

⚠Warning

The 60-vote cloture threshold is why Senate bills usually require bipartisan support. In a 51-49 Senate, the minority can simply refuse cloture and the bill dies — even if a simple majority supports it. This is the most important structural fact in modern Senate procedure.

Budget reconciliation is the major workaround: bills affecting federal revenue, spending, or the debt limit may be passed by simple majority, bypassing the filibuster. But reconciliation is constrained by the Byrd Rule — provisions must be primarily fiscal, not policy. The Senate Parliamentarian polices this and can strip non-compliant provisions.

Vote types

TypeHow it worksWhen used
Voice votePresiding officer calls aye/nay; judges by volumeRoutine, uncontested matters
DivisionMembers stand to be countedWhen voice vote is disputed
Roll call (Senate)Clerk calls each name; vote recordedSubstantive votes; on request
Electronic (House)Members swipe cards; tallied automaticallyMost votes in modern House

Summary

In this chapter you learned:

  • House: majoritarian; Rules Committee dictates debate; closed/structured rules dominate today.
  • Senate: filibuster default; 60 votes for cloture.
  • Budget reconciliation bypasses filibuster but is constrained by the Byrd Rule.
  • Four vote-recording types — voice, division, roll call, electronic.

Chapter 4

Second chamber + reconciliation

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
  • Explain why both chambers must pass identical bill text.
  • Describe the role of a conference committee.
  • Compare conference vs ping-pong reconciliation.

The identical-bill problem

After chamber A passes a bill, it is engrossed and sent to chamber B. B has four options:

  1. Pass the version as-is — rare; immediately enrolled and sent to President.
  2. Amend it — send back to A with B changes.
  3. Refuse to act — bill dies at the end of Congress.
  4. Substitute its own version — typical for major bills; triggers reconciliation.

The Constitution requires identical text from both chambers before a bill goes to the President. Differences must be reconciled.

Conference committee

Historically the standard reconciliation mechanism: leadership of both chambers appoints conferees (typically committee chairs + ranking members) to a temporary conference committee. Conferees negotiate a unified text, then each chamber votes up-or-down on the conference report — no further amendments allowed.

ℹNote

Conference reports are voted as a single take-it-or-leave-it package. This is enormous power for the conferees: they effectively rewrite the bill and the full chambers can only accept or reject the rewrite. Conferees often include provisions neither original chamber had passed (called "airdropped"). This has triggered ethics and transparency reforms in recent decades.

Ping-pong (amendments between chambers)

Increasingly, leadership skips the formal conference and uses "ping-pong": chamber A amends B amendment, sends back to B, which amends again, until both pass identical text. This concentrates power in leadership offices (Speaker + Majority Leader) rather than committee chairs.

✎Hands-on Exercise

Find a recent omnibus appropriations bill at congress.gov. Click "Actions." Look for the entry "Resolving differences — agreed to without amendment." That is the modern ping-pong endpoint. Compare to a bill from the 1990s — you will see "Conference report agreed to" instead.

Summary

In this chapter you learned:

  • The Constitution requires identical text from both chambers before the President can sign.
  • Conference committees are the historical reconciliation mechanism but rare today.
  • Ping-pong (mutual amendment) is the modern default, concentrating power in leadership.
  • Conference reports are voted take-it-or-leave-it — enormous power for conferees.

Chapter 5

President's desk — five fates

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
  • List the five outcomes when a bill reaches the President.
  • Explain why timing of Congressional adjournment matters (pocket veto).
  • Compute the override math: 2/3 of each chamber.
  • Identify why the line-item veto no longer exists.

The 10-day clock

Once Congress sends an enrolled bill to the President, the Constitution (Article I §7) gives him 10 days (excluding Sundays) to act. The clock starts when the bill is physically received at the White House.

Five possible outcomes

#ActionBecomes law?Notes
1SignYesStandard. Often accompanied by a signing statement.
2Veto + returnNo, unless overriddenReturned to the originating chamber with written objections.
3Pocket vetoNo10-day window expires while Congress is adjourned. No override possible.
4Allow without signatureYes10 days expire while Congress is in session. Symbolic protest.
5Line-item veto—Briefly existed (1996); struck down in Clinton v. City of New York (1998) as unconstitutional.

The pocket-veto trap

⚠Warning

The pocket veto is undeprivable. If Congress sends a bill late in a session and then adjourns sine die before the 10-day clock expires, the President can simply do nothing. The bill dies. There is no return-with-objections, no override opportunity — Congress is gone. This is why end-of-session bills are sent strategically.

Veto override math

\[ \text{Override} \iff (\text{House yes} \geq \tfrac{2}{3}) \land (\text{Senate yes} \geq \tfrac{2}{3}) \]
Override requires 2/3 of those present and voting in each chamber.

Historically fewer than 5% of vetoes are overridden. The 2/3 supermajority is the practical floor that makes the veto a strong tool.

Decision tree

Summary

In this chapter you learned:

  • Five outcomes — sign, veto, pocket veto, allow-without-signature, (defunct line-item veto).
  • Pocket veto requires Congressional adjournment during the 10-day window — no override possible.
  • Override math: 2/3 of each chamber; succeeds in fewer than 5% of attempts.
  • Line-item veto was struck down in Clinton v. City of New York (1998).

Chapter 6

After becoming law — rulemaking, courts, repeal

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:
  • Trace a statute from signature to U.S. Code codification.
  • Explain the APA notice-and-comment rulemaking process.
  • Identify the judicial-review pathways that can strike or narrow a statute.
  • Explain the three ways a law ends: repeal, sunset, judicial invalidation.

Public Law → U.S. Code

The moment the bill becomes law, it is assigned a Public Law number in the format P.L. CCC-NNN (e.g. P.L. 117-103 = Congress 117, statute 103). It is then:

  1. Published in Statutes at Large — chronological record of all enacted statutes.
  2. Codified into U.S. Code (USC) — the topical organization (Title 18 Crimes, Title 26 Tax, Title 42 Public Health). The Office of the Law Revision Counsel (House) maintains the USC.
  3. Indexed for cross-references and prior-law amendments.
ℹNote

Statutes at Large is the authoritative text. The USC is "prima facie" evidence of the law but in rare cases of conflict, courts defer to Statutes at Large. A small number of USC titles have been "enacted as positive law" — for those, the USC is itself authoritative.

Agency rulemaking under the APA

Statutes rarely contain enough detail to be self-executing. Congress typically delegates implementation to executive agencies, which write regulations under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA, 1946).

  1. Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM) — agency publishes proposed rule in the Federal Register.
  2. Public comment period — usually 30-60 days.
  3. Final rule — agency responds to substantive comments, publishes final text.
  4. Codified into Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), the regulatory parallel to USC.
★Best Practice

When tracing a real-world legal obligation, the chain is: Statute (USC) → Regulation (CFR) → Sub-regulatory guidance (agency manual). Each layer narrows interpretation. To know what a regulated entity must actually do, you read all three — not just the statute.

Judicial review

Article III courts can strike or narrow statutes via:

  • Facial challenge — the statute is unconstitutional on its face.
  • As-applied challenge — the statute is unconstitutional as applied to this litigant.
  • Statutory interpretation — narrow the meaning to avoid constitutional problems ("constitutional avoidance").
  • Pre-emption — federal law overrides conflicting state law (Supremacy Clause).

The Supreme Court is the final arbiter (since Marbury v. Madison, 1803).

💡Tip

"How a bill becomes a law" is the wrong question. The right question is: "How do 96% of bills die?" Once you internalize that, you understand why interest groups focus more on blocking bills than passing them — defense is much easier than offense in this system.
