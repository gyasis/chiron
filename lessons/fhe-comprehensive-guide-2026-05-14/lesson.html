Chapter 0

Cold open — why FHE matters

"Fully Homomorphic Encryption represents a paradigm shift in cryptography, enabling arbitrary computations on encrypted data without decryption." — FHE Guide §Executive Summary.

This lesson follows the FHE Comprehensive Guide from mathematical foundations to runnable code in Python, C++, and Rust. By the end you'll know when to reach for BGV vs BFV vs CKKS vs TFHE, which library matches your language, and what the noise/bootstrapping trade-off actually costs you.

Chapter 1

What is FHE — the problem and the trick

Before the timeline, the math, and the schemes — what IS Fully Homomorphic Encryption, and why does it exist?

The problem — Alice has data, Bob has compute, Alice doesn't trust Bob

Imagine you have a million patient records and you want to train a machine-learning model on them. You don't have the compute. Bob (AWS / Azure / a research collaborator) has the compute but you can't legally share the raw data with him.

What are your options?

  • Send the data unencrypted. Bob can compute, but Bob can read everything. ❌ HIPAA, GDPR, every privacy regulation.
  • Anonymize first. Doesn't work — re-identification attacks are trivial against rich tabular data.
  • Use MPC (multi-party computation). Real solution but communication-heavy — every operation is many network round-trips.
  • Encrypt then send. Now Bob has ciphertext and can't read it. But he also can't compute on it — encrypted data is gibberish to him.

This last one is the heart of the problem. Standard encryption (AES, RSA) destroys the ability to compute. Once your data is encrypted, it's an opaque blob. Bob can store it but he can't add two encrypted numbers to get an encrypted sum.

The trick — encryption that preserves arithmetic

Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) is an encryption scheme where the cipher's arithmetic mirrors the plaintext's arithmetic. Concretely:

  • You encrypt 5 → ciphertext C₅
  • You encrypt 3 → ciphertext C₃
  • You send C₅ and C₃ to Bob (Bob never sees 5 or 3)
  • Bob computes C_sum = C₅ + C₃ using FHE's "+"
  • Bob sends C_sum back to you
  • You decrypt C_sum → you get 8

Bob did the work. Bob never saw 5, never saw 3, never saw 8. You got the correct answer.

The same trick works for multiplication, and combining additions + multiplications lets you compute any function — including neural network inference, database queries, statistical aggregations.

A worked example — addition in BFV

Plaintext side:        Ciphertext side (what Bob sees):
─────────────────      ──────────────────────────────────
5                  →   C₅ = [4827, 9912, 7733, ..., 1024 ints]
3                  →   C₃ = [1199, 3402, 8821, ..., 1024 ints]
                       (each ciphertext is a polynomial with ~10⁴ integer coefficients)

You compute:           Bob computes (without seeing 5, 3, or 8):
  5 + 3 = 8              C_sum = C₅ + C₃ (just element-wise integer add)
                         C_sum = [6026, 13314, 16554, ..., 1024 ints]

Decrypt C_sum:
  8

Notice: the ciphertexts are thousands of times larger than the plaintexts (1024 coefficients × 60 bits each vs. one small integer). This expansion is the price of homomorphism — and the reason FHE is currently ~1,000× to 1,000,000× slower than plaintext compute.

Why this is hard — the bigger picture

For decades, cryptographers had only Partially Homomorphic Encryption (PHE):

  • RSA (1978) — preserves multiplication: RSA(a) × RSA(b) = RSA(a×b). Cannot add.
  • Paillier (1999) — preserves addition: Pail(a) × Pail(b) = Pail(a+b). Cannot multiply.

Each gives you ONE arithmetic operation, not both. Real computation needs both.

In 2009, Craig Gentry's PhD thesis (Stanford) introduced the first scheme that preserved BOTH addition and multiplication, on the same ciphertext, an arbitrary number of times. That's the "Fully" in FHE. The breakthrough technique was bootstrapping — a way to keep the noise that accumulates during operations under control. The earliest implementations were absurd (key sizes in gigabytes, single operations in seconds). Sixteen years of work later, we have practical FHE.

How FHE works — three operations and one budget

Every modern FHE scheme exposes the same three primitives:

  1. encrypt(plaintext) → ciphertext — same as any cipher, but the resulting ciphertext is a polynomial (or vector of polynomials), not just bits.
  2. add(C₁, C₂) → C₃ — homomorphic addition. Cheap (microseconds).
  3. multiply(C₁, C₂) → C₃ — homomorphic multiplication. Expensive (milliseconds).

The fourth operation — decrypt — is only run by the key holder at the very end.

The catch: every operation adds a small noise budget. Multiply two ciphertexts and the noise roughly squares. Once the noise exceeds a threshold, decryption returns garbage. Bootstrapping is the homomorphic decryption that resets the noise — at significant cost. The whole game of FHE engineering is "do as much real computation as you can before you're forced to bootstrap."

The rest of this lesson explains how each scheme implements these primitives, why each is suited to different tasks (BGV / BFV for integers, CKKS for floats, TFHE for booleans), and which libraries ship working implementations you can call from Python / C++ / Rust today.

  • 1978 — RSA + Paillier: partially homomorphic (one operation type only). Multiplicative (RSA) or additive (Paillier), never both on the same ciphertext.
  • 2009 — Craig Gentry's PhD thesis: first fully homomorphic scheme using ideal lattices + the critical insight of bootstrapping. Originally impractical (seconds per gate; absurd parameter sizes).
  • 2012 — BGV (Brakerski–Gentry–Vaikuntanathan) and BFV (Brakerski–Fan–Vercauteren): leveled FHE — bounded depth, no bootstrapping required. First parameter sizes a real machine could handle. RLWE replaces ideal lattices.

  • 2022 — OpenFHE unifies BGV / BFV / CKKS / TFHE / FHEW under one C++ library. One codebase per project, no more "we picked SEAL three years ago and now can't escape it."
  • 2024 — FINAL scheme: 28–33% faster bootstrapping vs prior TFHE variants. GPU + FPGA acceleration becomes production-grade.

Chapter 2

Mathematical foundations — LWE / RLWE / noise

Every modern FHE scheme rests on a hard lattice problem. The guide focuses on two:

LWE (Learning With Errors)

Given many (a, a·s + e) pairs where e is a small random error, recovering the secret s is conjectured intractable. Conjectured here means: no known polynomial-time algorithm, classical or quantum.

RLWE (Ring-LWE)

Same construction but over a polynomial ring. The win: a single ciphertext now encodes N values (typically N=2¹⁴ = 16,384), so one homomorphic operation operates on all 16k values in parallel. This is the SIMD batching property that makes FHE actually viable for ML.

The noise problem

Every operation adds noise to the ciphertext. Multiplications multiply the noise. Once noise exceeds the modulus bound, decryption returns garbage. Bootstrapping is the trick that resets the noise — it homomorphically applies the decryption function, producing a "fresh" ciphertext of the same plaintext.

Source: §2.1 (LWE), §2.2 (RLWE), §2.3 (noise + bootstrapping)

Practical pick guide

  • Microsoft SEAL (C++ / Python via sealpy) — BFV, BGV, CKKS. Easiest onboarding; clean docs, big community, the default for first-time FHE projects.
  • OpenFHE (C++ / Python via PyFHE) — all schemes in one library: BGV, BFV, CKKS, TFHE, FHEW. The production choice when you don't yet know which scheme you'll need and want to switch without rewriting.
  • IBM HElib (C++) — BGV, CKKS. Strong for advanced math operations, slot manipulation, deep multiplicative circuits.
  • Lattigo (Go) — cloud-service oriented; integrates cleanly with Go-based microservices.
  • TFHE-rs (Rust) — TFHE only, maintained by Zama. The Rust ecosystem's de facto choice.
  • TFHEpp (C++) — research-grade optimized TFHE.

Chapter 5

Performance — the bootstrapping cost dominates

FHE operations are 1,000× to 1,000,000× slower than plaintext. Most of that slowdown is bootstrapping. TFHE's per-gate bootstrap is ~13ms; BGV/BFV/CKKS bootstrapping is minutes.

PERFORMANCE BUDGET (relative to plaintext)
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Operation                        BGV/BFV       CKKS        TFHE
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Single addition                  ~10⁻⁵s        ~10⁻⁵s      ~10⁻⁶s
Single multiplication            ~10⁻³s        ~10⁻³s      n/a (uses gates)
Single boolean gate              n/a           n/a         ~13ms
Bootstrapping (refresh noise)    seconds-      seconds-    ~13ms
                                 minutes       minutes     (per gate)
Vs plaintext compute             1,000×        1,000×      1,000-
                                 -100,000×     -1,000,000× 100,000× slower

Notes:
• GPU acceleration: brings CKKS NN inference from minutes → seconds
• FPGA acceleration: 10-100× speedup demonstrated in academic prototypes
• 2024 FINAL scheme: 28-33% faster bootstrapping for TFHE-style schemes

Three fix paths the field is exploring in parallel:

  1. GPU acceleration — CKKS on consumer GPUs is now seconds-per-inference for small NNs. Intel HEXL provides SIMD primitives.
  2. FPGA + ASIC accelerators — real cryptographic-grade designs from Intel and Microsoft Research; 10-100× speedup demonstrated.
  3. Algorithmic gains — the 2024 FINAL scheme shaves 28-33% off bootstrap time at the math layer (no hardware change).

Net effect: a 2018 CKKS inference that took 30 minutes now takes ~10 seconds on a GPU + algorithmic stack. Still 10⁴× slower than plaintext, but in the "acceptable for non-realtime use cases" range (overnight batch jobs, asynchronous queries, healthcare reporting).

Chapter 6

Code examples — encrypt, compute, decrypt

The guide ships three runnable examples. All do the same skeleton: setup → keygen → encrypt → compute → decrypt. The differences are scheme-specific:

Microsoft SEAL — BFV, Python

# From §7.1 — SEAL BFV
# 1. Setup
parms = EncryptionParameters(scheme_type.bfv)
parms.set_poly_modulus_degree(8192)
parms.set_coeff_modulus(CoeffModulus.BFVDefault(8192))
parms.set_plain_modulus(1024)
context = SEALContext(parms)

# 2. Keys
keygen = KeyGenerator(context)
secret_key = keygen.secret_key()
public_key = keygen.create_public_key()

# 3. Encrypt → compute → decrypt
encryptor = Encryptor(context, public_key)
evaluator = Evaluator(context)
decryptor = Decryptor(context, secret_key)

ct1 = encryptor.encrypt(Plaintext('5'))
ct2 = encryptor.encrypt(Plaintext('3'))
ct_sum = evaluator.add(ct1, ct2)
print(decryptor.decrypt(ct_sum).to_string())  # → "8"

Chapter 7

Applications — where FHE is actually used

The guide's §6 enumerates concrete application domains:

Privacy-Preserving Machine Learning (PPML) — §6.2

Train models on encrypted patient data without ever seeing the raw values. Inference on encrypted user input ("does this image contain a tumor?") without the server learning the image. CKKS dominates here because neural networks are mostly multiply-add chains on floats.

Cross-Cloud Federated Learning (CCFL) — §6.3

Hospitals A, B, C all want to contribute to a joint diagnostic model but cannot share patient records. Each encrypts its gradient updates with the SAME FHE key; a federated aggregator sums the encrypted gradients; the master key holder decrypts the aggregate (never individual contributions). CKKS again.

Other application domains the source lists

  • Healthcare — encrypted genome / phenotype analysis
  • Finance — encrypted credit scoring, fraud detection on shared encrypted datasets
  • Cloud compute — outsource heavy computation to AWS / Azure without trusting them with the data
  • Government / census — population statistics without per-citizen disclosure
  • Encrypted database queries — search a server's data, server doesn't see query terms or results. BFV dominant.

The common shape: computation party ≠ data owner ≠ result consumer, and at least two of them don't trust each other.

🔬 Beyond the source — research expansion (Stage 2.5 demo)

This chapter was NOT in the source guide. It was added via a multi-hop research cascade — one anchor gemini_research call asked "what does a 2026 FHE guide typically under-cover?" and produced 5 expansion topics. This is a demonstration of Stage 2.5 enrichment — the gap chiron's pipeline currently has and that you correctly identified.

The source guide stops in 2024 and covers the math + schemes + libraries. Here's what the field has done since, that a serious learner needs:

1. Transciphering — making FHE viable for mobile / IoT

What: Client encrypts with a lightweight symmetric cipher (AES, FiLIP, Pasta); server homomorphically runs the symmetric decryption circuit to convert into an FHE ciphertext. Client never pays the 1000-10,000× ciphertext expansion cost.

Why it matters: Without transciphering, FHE is unusable for any bandwidth-constrained device.

2024-2026 fact: Apple deployed FHE for "Live Caller ID Lookup" in 2024. By 2025, transciphering for 8-bit precision reached <15ms latency — real-time consumer-app viable.

Sources: "Towards Practical Transciphering for FHE" (Geelen et al., 2025); OpenFHE transciphering modules.

2. Verifiable FHE — privacy AND integrity

What: Pair FHE computation with a zero-knowledge proof that the server actually ran the requested circuit (not garbage).

Why it matters: FHE gives privacy. It does NOT give integrity — a malicious server could return random output and you couldn't tell. ZKP+FHE closes this gap.

2024-2026 fact: The Zama fhEVM Mainnet launched in 2025 — first production-scale deployment where every FHE transaction is paired with a validity proof. Enables "Confidential DeFi" — private trades, publicly verifiable.

Sources: Sunscreen (ZKP+FHE compiler); Zama fhEVM; Gennaro et al., "Verifiable FHE".

3. Programmable bootstrapping — turning a tax into a feature

What: Evaluate a non-linear function (ReLU, sigmoid, lookup table) during the bootstrapping step, not as a separate operation. Pioneered in TFHE; recently extended to CKKS.

Why it matters: Bootstrapping is the dominant cost of FHE. Programmable bootstrapping turns it from "tax you pay to keep computing" into "compute step that also refreshes noise" — the secret sauce for deep-NN inference under encryption.

2024-2026 fact: OpenFHE shipped CKKS Functional Bootstrapping in early 2025 — 2-3 orders of magnitude higher throughput for batched ML tasks vs prior TFHE-based methods.

Sources: Zama Concrete; OpenFHE; Chillotti et al., "Programmable Bootstrapping".

4. Hardware co-design — the path to <10× overhead

What: Custom ASICs / FPGAs that treat 1024- or 2048-bit polynomial multiplications + Number Theoretic Transforms (NTT) as first-class primitives. Software-only FHE is ~10,000× slower than plaintext.

Why it matters: Without dedicated silicon, FHE stays research-only. Hardware acceleration is the bridge to production overhead targets.

2024-2026 fact: First commercial FHE ASICs (13-19nm) began shipping to data centers in Q4 2025 — 100-500× speedup over NVIDIA H100 GPUs for BGV/CKKS. Funded partly by DARPA DPRIVE.

Sources: DARPA DPRIVE program; BASALISC; ChainReaction; Optalysys (optical FHE).

5. Multi-key and threshold FHE — many parties, no single key holder

What: Multi-key FHE = compute on data encrypted under different keys. Threshold FHE = decryption requires a quorum of participants. Source guide assumes one key owner.

Why it matters: Real-world "data clean rooms" — hospital + pharma joint analytics — need multiple key holders, no single party with master decryption. Without multi-key/threshold variants, FHE doesn't solve this case.

2024-2026 fact: Multi-key BGV achieved linear complexity vs number of parties in 2025 — enabling first production collaborative-analytics platforms supporting up to 32 independent data providers per encrypted query.

Sources: Chen et al., "Multi-Key FHE from LWE, Revisited"; Boneh et al., "Threshold FHE"; Lattigo (strong multi-party support).
