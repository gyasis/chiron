Community-Acquired Pneumonia — Step 2 CK

Pneumonia is an acute infection of the lung parenchyma. CAP refers to cases arising outside hospital or long-term care. This chapter covers the Step-2-CK required arc: clinical features, diagnostics, and treatment — with epidemiology, complications, and prognosis as supporting context. Pathophysiology and etiology are touched lightly (Step 1 territory) but included as glossary depth.

AMBOSS sections covered: overviewepidemiologyetiologypathophysiologyclinical-featuresdiagnosticsdifferential-diagnosistreatmentcomplicationsprognosisreferences
Why you carePneumonia is one of the highest-yield Step 2 CK topics — it appears across IM, peds, and EM blocks. Knowing CURB-65 and the outpatient-vs-inpatient-vs-ICU decision distinguishes a passing answer from a stalled one.

Glossary

parenchyma
The functional tissue of the lung — alveoli and capillaries where gas exchange occurs (vs the stroma, which is structural connective tissue).
consolidation
Alveoli filled with exudate instead of air — appears as opaque density on chest X-ray.
egophony
When the patient says 'eee', it sounds like 'aaa' on auscultation — a sign of consolidation.
V/Q mismatch
Ventilation-perfusion mismatch — alveoli are perfused but not ventilated (or vice versa), causing hypoxemia.
atypical pneumonia
Pneumonia caused by Mycoplasma, Chlamydophila, or Legionella — typically subacute, dry cough, often extrapulmonary symptoms.
CURB-65
Severity score: Confusion, Urea>7mmol/L, RR≥30, BP<90/60, age≥65. 0-1 outpatient, 2 admit, 3-5 ICU.

Epidemiology at a glance

1
Incidence

~5-6 cases per 1,000 adults per year in the US. Over 1 million hospitalisations annually.

2
At-risk

Highest incidence in <2y and >65y. Smokers, COPD, asthma, immunosuppression, recent viral URI.

3
Mortality

Outpatient <1%. Admitted 5-15%. ICU mortality varies with severity and timely abx.

4
Top pathogen

Streptococcus pneumoniae remains the most common typical pathogen across age groups.

Pathogens — typical vs atypical vs viral

Typical

Bacterial typical

Strep. pneumoniae · Haemophilus influenzae · Moraxella catarrhalis. Classic lobar pattern, productive cough, high fever.

Often urinary antigen positive (Pneumococcus)
Atypical

Bacterial atypical

Mycoplasma · Chlamydophila · Legionella. Subacute course, dry cough, headache, GI symptoms. Walking pneumonia.

Treat with macrolide or fluoroquinolone
Viral

Viral

Influenza A/B · SARS-CoV-2 · RSV · adenovirus. Often precedes a secondary bacterial infection.

Antiviral (oseltamivir, paxlovid) if early

Clinical features — typical presentation

Hx

History

Acute onset fever, productive cough (yellow/green sputum), pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea on exertion. Recent viral URI common.

Atypical: subacute, dry cough, headache
PE

Physical exam

Tachypnea (RR>20), tachycardia, fever. Crackles on auscultation. Egophony, increased fremitus, dullness to percussion over consolidation.

Atypical PE often normal-sounding lung
Vit

Vitals red flags

RR ≥30, SBP <90, HR >120, SpO2 <90% on room air, AMS. Any one triggers admission consideration.

CURB-65 — one point each

1
C — Confusion

New-onset altered mental status. Often subtle in elderly.

2
U — Urea

BUN > 19 mg/dL (urea > 7 mmol/L). Marker of dehydration / severity.

3
R — Respiratory rate

≥ 30 breaths/min. Indicator of respiratory compromise.

4
B — Blood pressure

SBP < 90 OR DBP ≤ 60. Hemodynamic instability.

Step 0 / 5

Empiric antibiotic ladder

1
Outpatient (healthy)

Amoxicillin OR doxycycline OR azithromycin (macrolide). 5-7 days.

2
Outpatient (comorbid)

Amoxicillin/clavulanate + macrolide, OR respiratory fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin).

3
Inpatient non-ICU

Beta-lactam (ceftriaxone) + macrolide, OR respiratory fluoroquinolone monotherapy.

4
ICU

Beta-lactam + macrolide OR + fluoroquinolone. Add anti-MRSA (vancomycin/linezolid) and/or anti-Pseudomonas (piperacillin/tazobactam) if risk factors.

Complications — what goes wrong

C1

Parapneumonic effusion → empyema

Fluid collection adjacent to pneumonia. If infected (empyema) requires drainage in addition to antibiotics.

Thoracentesis if effusion > 1cm on decub
C2

Lung abscess

Cavitary lesion with air-fluid level on imaging. Anaerobic coverage often needed.

Risk: aspiration, alcoholism, poor dentition
C3

Sepsis / septic shock

Most common cause of CAP mortality. Recognise early via SIRS/qSOFA, treat per sepsis bundle.

qSOFA ≥2 → high risk
C4

ARDS

Bilateral infiltrates, PaO2/FiO2 < 300, requiring mechanical ventilation. ICU course.

Lung-protective ventilation strategy
A 68-year-old man with COPD on tiotropium presents to the ED with 3 days of productive cough, fever to 38.9°C, and pleuritic right-sided chest pain. RR 28, BP 102/64, HR 110, SpO2 92% on room air. Crackles and egophony in the right lower lung field. WBC 16,400 with left shift, BUN 22 mg/dL, creatinine 1.1. CXR shows right lower lobe consolidation. He is alert and oriented.
Age 68 (≥65 → +1 CURB-65) BUN 22 mg/dL (>19 → +1 CURB-65) RR 28 (<30 → 0) BP 102/64 (>90 SBP, >60 DBP → 0) Alert and oriented (no confusion → 0) CURB-65 = 2 Has COPD comorbidity
What is the next best step in management?
  1. Wrong — CURB-65 of 2 indicates admission. Outpatient amoxicillin alone is insufficient even setting CURB aside (COPD comorbidity).
  2. Correct — CURB-65 = 2 (age + BUN) supports admission. Standard inpatient non-ICU empiric therapy is beta-lactam + macrolide.
  3. Overtreatment — CURB-65 = 2, no MRSA/Pseudomonas risk factors, hemodynamically stable. Reserve broad coverage for ICU-level severity with risk factors.
  4. PE not consistent with clear consolidation + productive cough + fever + leukocytosis. CXR findings + clinical picture establish CAP.
  5. Levofloxacin is correct outpatient option for COPD comorbidity, BUT CURB-65 = 2 mandates admission. The drug choice is right; the disposition is wrong.

Attending Tip — Dr. Reyes:

Step 2 CK loves combining CURB-65 with comorbidity. Always compute CURB-65 explicitly — the score determines disposition, comorbidity guides drug class.

Prognosis — what to tell the patient

1
Outpatient

<1% 30-day mortality. Most improve in 48-72 hours of appropriate abx.

2
Inpatient ward

5-10% in-hospital mortality. Older / comorbid trend higher.

3
ICU

15-30% mortality, higher with shock or ARDS. Mortality drops with timely (≤1hr from suspicion) antibiotics.

4
Long-term

Cough can persist 4 weeks; fatigue often longer. Pulmonary fibrosis is rare. Reinfection risk slightly elevated for 12 months.
